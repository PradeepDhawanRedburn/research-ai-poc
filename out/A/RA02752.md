Pop Britain - Site Visit

CCEP hosted a site visit in Great Britain – its largest market for operating income. This reinforced our positive view on a business unit that has delivered outstanding c5% sales growth pa over the past decade despite the pandemic, challenges in the away-from-home channel and the introduction of a UK sugar tax. CCEP trades on 17.6x forward P/E, which is attractive for a stock that offers a c10% EPS CAGR over the next three years with easing commodity costs and less downside risk versus staples peers.

Great Britain is 16% of CCEP’s sales and, we estimate, the largest contributor to operating at c21% of the total. CCEP hosted an excellent site visit to its Wakefield production plant (the largest soft drinks plant in Europe) and a market visit to at-home and away-from-home (AFH) outlets to witness the improved execution.

In this report we discuss our key learnings, such as the investment made at Wakefield to boost efficiency and improve margins. Capacity is being added: necessary given the British soft drinks market has grown at c4% pa over the past decade and Coca-Cola Great Britain’s (CCGB) sales grew even faster at 5.2% pa, on our estimates. The highlight of the market tour for us was a visit to a wholesaler, which demonstrated how CCGB’s field sales team drive sales growth with a highly fragmented end-customer base, as well as how wholesalers support CCEP’s ambition to place c100,000 coolers in the AFH channel across its markets, which should lead to faster sales growth of the higher margin single-serve packs.

We also discuss the evolving landscape of the British soft drinks market, namely Britvic’s acquisition by Carlsberg (Buy rated) and the onslaught of regulation that, while burdensome, could cement the position of the large incumbents. Britvic’s key products were deeply discounted ahead of the acquisition, but the innovation launches in 2025 are encouraging and should expand the industry’s profit pool. Meanwhile, the upcoming UK deposit return scheme (DRS) in October 2027 could pose challenges to multipacks of cans, requiring both CCGB and Carlsberg-Britvic to reassess their respective price/pack architecture.

Our CCEP estimates are unchanged and we continue to forecast a c10% EPS CAGR over the next three years with a c3% dividend yield. This is broadly in line with consensus. CCEP trades on 17.6x one-year forward P/E; a 5% discount to its pre-pandemic average, despite offering faster top- and bottom-line growth today, with high free cash flow delivery. With potential margin upside in 2026/27 from falling sugar and plastic costs, and less downside risk versus the broader sector, we maintain our Buy rating on CCEP with an unchanged $102/share target price.

Brief British history

Great Britain has a longstanding affinity towards soft drinks, starting with the arrival of cordials from Italy in the 15th century. In 1767, British chemist Joseph Priestly discovered a process for carbonating water that he published in a 1772 paper *Directions for* *Impregnating Water with Fixed Air*. Building on Priestly’s work, a young Swiss watchmaker named Johann Jacob Schweppe developed the first commercial process for producing carbonated mineral water in Geneva in 1783. His new company – Schweppes Company – expanded into Great Britain in 1792, producing soda water for medicinal purposes at a small factory on Drury Lane in London. In 1835, Schweppes Company launched a lemonade, kickstarting a wave of copycat competitors (e.g. R White’s in 1845, now part of Britvic), before expanding into ginger ale (1870) and the eponymous tonic water (1871).

Coca-Cola was a relative latecomer, first introduced to Great Britain in 1900 by the son of Asa Candler (who incorporated *The* Coca-Cola Company (TCCC) in 1892) and sold in Spence’s Department store next to St Paul’s Cathedral. Local Coca-Cola bottling began in Brighton in 1932 and 1,250 cases were sold in the first year of operation. This rapidly rose to seven plants selling 90,000 cases by the end of the Second World War, but over the following decades the British network remained fragmented and Coca-Cola bottling was performed by partners that were not focused on soft drinks, such as Grand Metropolitan (now Diageo (Neutral rated)). Paired with an increasingly competitive local market, this meant that per capita consumption of TCCC’s beverages in Great Britain remained low.

In 1981, Roberto Goizueta was appointed CEO of TCCC. One of his many legacies was strengthening TCCC’s international business and at the time Great Britain was an outlier – despite the territory’s soft drinks heritage, Great Britain’s per capita consumption was below both the world average and other Anglosphere markets like the US and Australia (Fig 3).

This presented TCCC with a clear growth opportunity and on 1 June 1984 it acquired Grand Metropolitan’s Coca-Cola bottling business and then began consolidating the bottling network in Great Britain into a single bottler – Amalgamated Beverages.

In January 1987, TCCC merged Amalgamated Beverages with the UK soft drinks business of Cadbury Schweppes plc (Cadbury merged with Schweppes in 1969), forming a joint venture called Coca-Cola & Schweppes Beverages (CCSB) (Fig 4). This combined the Coca-Cola portfolio with the Schweppes brand, Appletiser, Oasis, Capri-Sun, Rose’s cordials and Kia-Ora.

CCSB accounted for one-third of total UK soft drinks market volumes in 1987 and its formation transformed the UK soft drinks market.

Disrupting the competitor

Cadbury Schweppes had been a Pepsi bottler for over 20 years in Great Britain and its switch to Coca-Cola in 1987 prompted PepsiCo to consolidate and strengthen its own UK bottling network into a single bottler – Britvic – which acquired the soft drinks brands of Beecham Group[[1]](#footnote-2) (Tango etc) in 1987 to create a scaled soft drinks competitor with a 20% volume share of the UK soft drinks market.

As part of the licensing deal, PepsiCo acquired a 10% stake in Britvic from its owner Britannia Soft Drinks; a division of brewer Allied-Lyons that was created in 1980 by merging the soft drinks businesses of Allied Breweries, Whitbread and Bass Brewery. This deal strengthened Pepsi’s access to the AFH channel – Bass owned what is now Mitchells & Butlers, one of the UK’s largest managed pub operators, while Whitbread owned 1,600 managed pubs as well as franchises for Pizza Hut and T.G.I. Fridays – and created a stronger cola competitor to Coca-Cola.

Keep calm and drink cola

As such, we tend to focus on the UK soft drinks market from 1987 given it largely reflects the competitor dynamics of today.

CCSB’s greater scale, focus and investment drove significant growth for the overall soft drinks category – especially for the cola category – since 1987, we estimate that Trademark Coca-Cola has grown at a 2% volume CAGR (Fig 5).

This growth was underpinned by a major capital expenditure programme that CCSB started upon its creation, including the commissioning of the Wakefield production plant. Wakefield was (and still is) the largest soft drinks plant in Europe and once this new mega plant ramped up production in 1990, CCSB then closed two older plants to unlock cost savings, which were partially reinvested behind top-line growth initiatives. Growth was also supported by higher marketing spend by TCCC, including the iconic 1995 UK “Diet Coke Break” advert – today the UK is still the second largest market for Diet Coke behind the US.

Meanwhile, Britvic raised the competitive intensity in the overall soft drinks industry. This was particularly acute in the cola category, helped by the successful launch of the sweeter tasting, sugar free Pepsi Max in 1993. Trademark Pepsi (Pepsi, Diet Pepsi, Pepsi Max etc) gradually started to win volume share from Trademark Coca-Cola (Classic, Diet, Zero Sugar etc), helped by affordability challenges in the Global Financial Crisis (Pepsi generally retails at a discount to Coca-Cola) and by upscaling the on-the-go Pepsi Max format from a 500ml to a 600ml PET bottle in 2010 but keeping the price the same (a de facto price cut on a per 100ml basis). Since 1987, we estimate that Trademark Pepsi grew at a 5% CAGR in Great Britain, faster than Trademark Coca-Cola, albeit off a smaller base.

Britvic was acquired by Carlsberg (Buy rated) in January 2025 as part of the Danish brewer’s strategy to expand beyond beer. We discuss some of the potential implications for CCEP later in this report.

Changing hands

Despite a healthy rivalry with Britvic in the cola category, we estimate that Coca-Cola Great Britain (CCGB) has averaged 5.3% organic sales growth since 1988, although growth skews to the earlier years when the business was operated by CCSB (Fig 6).

Coca-Cola Enterprises (CCE) acquired CCGB in 1997 as part of the then North American bottler’s plans to diversify internationally, and TCCC committed to a five-year upweighted marketing plan to support strong volume growth from 1997-2002.

This drove mid-single-digit growth for six consecutive years, as shown in Fig 7. But thereafter CCGB’s volume growth faced challenges, such as in February 2004 when it infamously expanded into the bottled water category with Dasani, only to discontinue the brand in March 2004 following poor consumer reception and a product recall. The launches of Coca-Cola Zero Sugar (2005) and Monster Energy (2008) helped spur volume growth to 2010, when TCCC acquired the US operations of CCE as part of its North American bottler refranchising – discussed in ‘[Super System](https://redburn.rothschildandco.com/pdf/45236?title=Super+System&pages=124)’ (8 September 2023).

Thereafter, CCE became a Europe-only bottler, but despite this heightened focus it struggled to combat the rise of Pepsi Max in the 2010s, contributing to CCGB’s weak organic sales growth performance in 2012-15.

CCE’s performance was not much better in its other European markets (France, Benelux, the Netherlands, Norway and Sweden) and in May 2016, CCE was combined with Coca-Cola Iberian Partners (Spain and Portugal) and Coca-Cola Erfrischungsgetränk AG (Germany) to form Coca-Cola European Partners (CCEP, now Coca-Cola Europacific Partners).

CCEP then began to rejuvenate the business and since 2016, on our estimates, CCGB has averaged 1.8% volume growth pa, while revenue per case has risen at 4% pa, leading to 5.8% organic sales growth pa. This is impressive given the challenging backdrop that the UK has faced during CCEP’s tenure:

* In April 2018, the UK government introduced a soft drinks industry sugar levy (SDIL). This taxed beverages with added sugar, leading to an industry-wide shift from sugar-sweetened sparkling beverages to zero-sugar options, which dented volumes for market leader Coca-Cola Classic in the cola category (Fig 8). Pepsi Max was the main beneficiary, supported by additional production capacity in late 2019 after Britvic completed its supply chain investment plan termed the ‘Business Capability Programme’. Since then, Pepsi Max has consistently won volume share from Coca-Cola Classic and Diet Coke (although the latter has now stabilised in 3Q25), while Coca-Cola Zero Sugar has consistently gained volume share, helped by the successful 2021 reformulation by TCCC;
* CO2 shortages have threatened carbonated soft drinks production in Great Britain several times, most acutely in 2018 and 2021;
* The pandemic and slow reopening in 2020-21 restricted footfall in the AFH channel, which in 2019 was 46% of Coca-Cola Great Britain’s volumes. The AFH channel then faced ongoing challenges post-pandemic, such as high inflation from 2022 and adverse government policies at the 2024 Autumn Budget, including an increase to the minimum wage and employer National Insurance contributions, and reduced business rates relief;
* Widespread inflation following the 2022 war in Ukraine led to both higher input costs for CCGB and a weaker consumer environment.

Alongside this, CCEP’s portfolio pruning of Kia-Ora (2019-20) and Capri-Sun (2024-25) held back volume growth in the affected years, although this should modestly improve the territory’s long-term growth profile. Today, CCGB focuses on two categories: sparkling beverages and energy drinks, which together are 93% of total volumes (Fig 9). This is in contrast to the more balanced category profile of the total UK soft drinks market, but it is worth noting that CCEP does not operate in the juice category because TCCC does via the innocent brand, while in water CCEP has made the strategic decision to focus on small single-serve packs of Smartwater that earn a better margin.

The British footprint

CCGB has a production footprint that is little changed over the past 30 years, apart from the closure of the Milton Keynes plant in 2019 (Fig 10). CCGB’s five production plants are strategically located close to key cities and near to major motorways to improve their distribution reach.

| Fig 10: Coca-Cola Great Britain production footprint |
| --- |
| ![](image) |
| Source: Rothschild & Co Redburn |

Edmonton and Sidcup surround Britain’s biggest city by population, London, and are close to the M25 motorway. Wakefield is the largest plant at c45% of CCGB’s volumes and it is positioned at the crossroads of the M1 and M62 so that it can service Leeds and Manchester (the third and fifth biggest cities respectively by population) as well as Birmingham (second biggest city) and even London.

Meanwhile, the Morpeth plant is the smallest with one production line that produces solely water under the Glaceau Smartwater and Abbey Well brands for the whole of Great Britain. Morpeth came from TCCC’s acquisition of Abbey Well in 2008, which as CCE described in its 10-K that year was *“an important first step in creating a viable water strategy in Great Britain”* following the failure of Dasani*.* The strategy involved heavy marketing and activation for Abbey Well to accompany the London 2012 Olympics, butas shown in Fig 9, this never led to a sustained improvement for CCGB’s water volumes.

There are similar nuances elsewhere: the Edmonton plant produces all of the post-mix syrup for Great Britain and East Kilbride produces all of the Appletiser glass bottle formats. Sidcup is the oldest plant (opened in 1961 by Schweppes) and it focuses on smaller pack formats; it is the only site that produces the 150ml and 250ml ‘mini cans’.

Site visit: Wakefield production plant

When the Wakefield plant was completed in 1989 it housed two canning lines and two PET lines. It was built adjacent to Nacanco’s aluminium can manufacturing plant, which supplied Wakefield with aluminium cans through a ‘hole in the wall’, providing Wakefield with a cost advantage compared to competitors’ plants.

In the years since, Wakefield has expanded to 11 production lines plus an Automated Storage Retrieval System (ASRS, effectively a large, automated warehouse). Wakefield has received consistent investment over the years, including over £100m since 2019, within which £42m is to build a new ASRS (‘ASRS 2’ in Fig 11) that will double Wakefield’s onsite storage capacity to 58,500 pallets when it is completed (due in 2026). CCEP hosted an excellent site visit to the Wakefield plant and a market tour around Leeds on 18 November 2025. Wakefield is the company’s second largest plant by volumes after the Canlubang plant in the Philippines, which we also visited earlier in the year, see ‘[From Manila to Jakarta](https://redburn.rothschildandco.com/pdf/70391?title=From+Manila+to+Jakarta&pages=37)’ (8 July 2025).

The site visit showcased where all this investment had been spent: rows of state-of-the-art manufacturing lines (including one of the two Monster Energy drinks lines in Great Britain), robotic automatic palletising machines, a 38 metre tall ASRS that seamlessly stores and retrieves thousands of pallets, and more (Fig 12).

Like Canlubang, all the employees that we encountered at the Wakefield plant were passionate about their role and highly knowledgeable about their domain. One aspect that impressed us was the future-proofing at the plant; not just building a new ASRS to help meet future demand, but a local hiring programme to train young engineers to counter any potential ‘cliff edge’ when c100-150 more senior employees retire in future (many of whom joined the plant back in 1989).

The British soft drinks market

Before discussing the Leeds market visit it is worth setting the scene by discussing the British soft drinks industry.

CCGB’s strong route-to-market underpins its leadership in a soft drinks category that we estimate is worth just over £21bn in retail value and has grown at just over a c4% CAGR over the past decade (Fig 13). The largest channel is grocery (i.e. supermarket chains like Tesco) at 48% of total soft drinks value, while the AFH channel – convenience stores, licensed outlets (hotels, pubs, restaurant) and food service (quick-service restaurants, leisure and travel) – is 52%.

| Fig 13: Keep Calm and Carry On |
| --- |
| ![](image) |
| Source: Rothschild & Co Redburn, Nielsen CGA, Circana, Kantar |

In the five years prior to the pandemic, the British soft drinks market grew at c3.5% pa, balanced between volume growth and average realised price (ARP) growth, which is what we expect it will grow at in future (Fig 14). The 2020 pandemic led to a c-8% volume contraction, on our estimates, with a c-14% ARP decline due to the negative mix effect of volumes shifting to the lower revenue-per-litre grocery channel, coupled with private label then winning share from branded players within the grocery channel.

Volumes sold through the licensed and food service channels have not recovered to pre-pandemic levels due to the constant challenges faced by the UK hospitality industry since 2020 – lockdown closures and a slow reopening, high inflation (particularly labour, food and energy) since 2022, and tax hikes in 2024 (Fig 15).

The out-of-home recovery story…

The AFH channel definition includes convenience stores which have performed well since the pandemic – volumes are up 9% versus 2019 and the channel has risen from a 13% value share of total soft drinks in 2019 to 16% in 2024. However, it is unclear whether drinks purchased in convenience stores are immediately consumed, or whether they are brought home for future consumption.

As such, we also look at the ‘out-of-home’ (OOH) channel (a subset of the AFH channel) where beverages are consumed immediately. This is the combined performance of the licensed and food service channels and where there has been the most pressure in recent years – 2024 volumes sold in the OOH channel are -10% compared to 2019 and the OOH channel has dropped from 46% of total soft drinks value in 2019 to 36% in 2024, with the grocery channel picking up much of the gains and rising from 42% to 48% of total industry value over the same period.

We suspect that most of the weakness in the OOH channel is linked to near-term affordability issues – for example, numerous quick-service-restaurant operators like McDonalds’s (Sell rated) raised prices aggressively in 2022, hitting footfall and same-store-sales growth, and are now back peddling to improve their affordable menu offering. The only sub-channel where we see structural pressure is in the nightclubs (c9%/c3% of OOH/total Great Britain soft drinks value) as following the pandemic younger consumers have shunned socialising with strangers in dark clubs in favour of healthier lifestyles and visiting the gym.

For CCGB, there is potentially a significant price/mix growth opportunity if the OOH channel recovers. For context, soft drinks sold through the licensed channel earn an ARP that is 3.2x that of the market average and 4.4x that of the grocery channel (Fig 16). Meanwhile, soft drinks sold through food service earn an ARP that is 24% higher than the market average.

We are not holding our breath ahead of the 2025 Autumn budget on 26 November, which could further dampen the consumer environment if, as widely expected, tax hikes are on the way. As discussed later in the market visit section, CCGB is not sitting idly waiting for the OOH channel to recover.

If and when the OOH channel recovers, CCGB is well positioned to capitalise – we estimate that it holds a 46% and 47% value share of the total soft drinks category in the licensed and food service channels respectively, well ahead of the number two player Britvic (27% and 14% respectively) (Fig 17).

German discounters versus private equity

The UK’s third largest grocer, Asda, was acquired by the Issa brothers and private equity firm TDR Capital in February 2021. Then in October 2021, the fourth largest grocer, Morrisons, was bought by private equity firm Clayton, Dubiler & Rice. As shown in Fig 21, both grocers have struggled since being taken private: Asda has lost 250bp of market share since 2020 while Morrisons has lost 200bp. Aldi and Lidl have been the main beneficiaries, both winning 250bp of market share since 2020.

Morrisons is in a tough spot having been levered up to extract dividends while concurrently failing to improve its value offering. In July 2025, Fitch forecasted that Morrisons would end FY25E on 6.2x net-debt EBITDAR[[4]](#footnote-5), falling modestly to just below 6.0x in FY26E. Fitch also downgraded Morrisons’ debt rating from 'BB' to 'BB-' i.e. one notch above CCC, the grade that signals “substantial risk” of default.

Morrisons announced price cuts on c650 products in September 2025 and in October 2025 it announced plans to shut 145 sites (c8% of the total store footprint) as part of an ongoing cost cutting programme. We will have to see wait and see if this is enough because, following suit, in October 2025 Asda announced price cuts on over 960 products in an attempt to win back market share.

Despite these countermeasures, we would expect Aldi and Lidl to continue winning share from Asda and Morrisons. In our coverage, Morrisons’ challenges pose the most risk to AG Barr (Buy rated), which is relatively stronger in the North of England (where Asda and Morrisons have a higher market share compared to the South of England). CCGB should be relatively less affected, but nevertheless, the structural shift towards Aldi and Lidl could create a few challenges.

What does the shift to discounters mean for CCGB?

From a margin perspective, the shift from traditional grocers to discounters could create gross margin pressure for CCGB. This stems firstly from the lower revenue per case that CCGB earns from selling to the discounters, and secondly from the ‘price matches’ that could limit pricing power with the traditional grocers.

It is difficult to glean much information from the statutory accounts of the impact of the shift to discounters because the gross margin has been knocked repeatedly by the 2018 UK sugar tax, the 2020 pandemic and then ongoing inflation following the 2022 war in Ukraine (Fig 25). Furthermore, in 2025 the UK sugar tax was increased, leading to more gross margin pressure.

If pricing power is more limited due to the rise of discounters, then improving the gross margin will depend on improving the cost base at CCGB. The statutory accounts imply that concentrate is c33% of cost of goods sold (COGS) in Great Britain and this is largely fixed due to the bottler agreement with TCCC. But for the remaining two-thirds of COGS, CCGB can improve its cost base to drive gross margin expansion – as discussed on page 10, the new warehouse being built at Wakefield should help in this regard.

The operating margin should not face as much pressure from the shift to the discount channel as although CCGB sells to the discounter at a lower gross margin, operating expenses are also lower as products are shipped to the distributors’ warehouses and merchandised by their in-store clerks.

We therefore suspect that CCGB’s operating margin in the discount channel is not vastly lower than its average in the at-home channel, which as discussed in ‘[Europe Emerging](https://redburn.rothschildandco.com/pdf/65615?title=Europe+Emerging&pages=18)’ (10 March 2025) we estimate was 12.8% in Europe in 2019 compared to the region’s average of 13.9% and the European AFH channel average of 15.4%.

Market visit: tour around Leeds

One of the key elements behind the improved execution both at CCGB and across the wider CCEP is the increasing power of the company’s data and analytics tools. These were discussed in detail at CCEP’s 2025 Capital Markets Event in Manila, so we will not repeat here apart from the Right Execution Daily (RED) One application.

Each of CCGB’s c800 field sales team has an iPad with the RED One app. This is a proprietary app that improves in-store execution by providing the field sales team with store-level planograms and data and analytics tools to help review each store’s performance and suggest actions for improvement. The tool can also help to help optimise routes, maximising the field sales rep’s productive time in store and minimising the unproductive time travelling between stores. It is an incredibly powerful tool and has helped CCEP to strengthen its relationships with customers, both through selling more soft drinks but also through selling more of the customer’s products – for example, RED One often suggests initiatives such as placing Coca-Cola products adjacent to a retailer’s food products, which leads to greater sales of both products.

The RED One application then scores each store out of 100 using a variety of metrics – the higher the REDScore, the better the overall execution. This introduces a gamification aspect to the field sales role, making the job more engaging (important for talent retention) with improvements to the REDScore tied to incentives.

The road ahead

The market tour reinforced our view that CCGB’s execution has made a step change improvement since the days of CCE. The local culture has been transformed and it was notable that the field sales team is now ‘playing to win’ rather than the old defensive mindset in the 2010s as CCE struggled to combat the rise of Pepsi Max under Britvic.

Going forwards, the local operating environment in Great Britain is unlikely to ease and, in our view, the two biggest risks to CCGB’s long-term sales growth come from aggressive competitor behaviour and further government policy changes.

Implications of Carlsberg-Britvic

In January 2025, CCGB’s main competitor and the Pepsi bottler in the UK, Britvic, was acquired by Carlsberg to create Carlsberg-Britvic, which is now the largest multi-beverage supplier in the UK. Before discussing the impact this could have on the British soft drinks market, the most pressing question we have received from investors regards the cost savings plan.

Upon acquisition, Carlsberg announced a £100m cost savings plan for Carlsberg-Britvic to the end of 2029, equivalent to 6% of Britvic’s sales, from sources such as procurement, supply chain and administration and overheads.

Before the acquisition we covered Britvic with a Buy rating and we would have struggled to envisage cost savings of this magnitude were possible without exiting parts of the business and without jeopardising front-line execution, especially after the company underwent a £240m supply chain transformation (termed the ‘Business Capability Programme’ (BCP)) in Great Britain (two-thirds of Britvic’s total revenue) from 2015-19 with the aim of optimising and future-proofing the business. This was a vast programme that included commissioning ten new, more efficient production lines, shifting production from Norwich to Rugby to reduce logistics costs, upgrading the existing warehouses, and building a new, fully automated high-bay warehouse at Rugby, and more.

Then, at its October 2025 Capital Markets Day, Carlsberg raised the cost savings target from £100m to £110m (7% of Britvic’s sales). We suspect that part of this increase is due to the planned closure of the Teisseire plant in France and the decision to outsource production to Slaur-Sardet; a copacker for Teisseire since 2022. This will unlock cost savings, but it will not solve Teisseire's top-line issues: Britvic’s largest French brand had been in decline for a decade with softening volumes and pricing that was insufficient to cover inflation, leading to worsening profitability. We would not be surprised if Carlsberg were to review the broader legacy Britvic France business.

In our view, this cost savings plan could create an opportunity for CCGB if, for example, Carlsberg-Britvic loses key talent which negatively impacts its execution, or if Carlsberg-Britvic loses focus as it tries to deliver the sizeable cost savings programme, which would have the same outcome.

We have already seen the former happen and talent is leaving the legacy Britvic business for competitors – AG Barr has been a beneficiary and hired Dino Labbate as the new Chief Commercial Officer (previously Britvic’s GB Commercial Director for Hospitality) and James Quemby as Retail Sales Director (previously Grocery Customer Director at Britvic i.e. the main driver in the grocery channel behind Ben Parker, who remains at Carlsberg-Britvic as VP Sales – Off Trade).

On the latter, we will have to wait and see – it is rare in the consumer staples sector to see a company deliver both cost savings and accelerating top-line growth. Currently, there is little clarity on the specifics of Carlsberg’s £110m cost savings plan, other than the high-level sources already outlined.

Conversely, the main risk to CCEP is that the new Carlsberg-Britvic entity, helped by a stronger strategic partnership with PepsiCo, creates a more capable competitor in Great Britain.

Wins and losses in the OOH channel

As discussed in ‘[The Juggernaut](https://research.redburnatlantic.com/pdf/2384?title=The%2520Juggernaut)’ (4 November 2022), PepsiCo always struggled in the out-of-home channel relative to TCCC and it acquired KFC, Pizza Hut and Taco Bell in the 1970s partly to secure end-customers for its products. PepsiCo spun off these operators in 1997 to form Tricon, now Yum! Brands (Buy rated).

However, the US challenges never held back Britvic, which developed a competent in-house dispense solutions business in the 2000s, strengthened by the 2020 acquisition of The Boiling Tap Company. A classic example was the Britvic-patented Pepsi Xtra Cold dispenser, launched in the late-2000s across Mitchells & Butlers’ 2,000 outlets (Fig 43). This dispenser never took off, partly because it launched in the midst of the GFC, but in October 2025 Carlsberg-Britvic is back with a new ‘Halo’ dispenser that it hopes can turn the tide against Coca-Cola in post-mix (Fig 44).

This new dispenser could be necessary as although the combination of Carlsberg and Britvic adds scale that is useful for customer negotiations in the grocery channel, it can create friction in the more profitable out-of-home channel.

For example, Heineken (Buy rated) owns c2,400 pubs around the UK through Star Pubs: would it be happy to list Pepsi products, thereby helping one of its beer competitors, Carlsberg? Before the acquisition this would not have been an issue for Britvic, yet we have already seen an example of this potential issue in action. Britvic lost the Pepsi distribution agreement with Fuller, Smith & Turner (c400 pubs) to CCGB in 2025. Meanwhile, Carlsberg-Britvic won a distribution agreement with St Austell (c160 pubs) from CCGB, but clearly this will not fully offset the lost Fuller, Smith & Turner outlets.

Net-net, this is a win for Coca-Cola and a loss for Pepsi, that perhaps would not have happened previously. CCGB could win further outlets in the licensed channel following Carlsberg’s acquisition of Britvic and/or it could make it easier for CCGB to renew existing agreements (for example, CCGB’s agreement with Stonegate was recently renewed). However, we doubt that long-standing Pepsi vendor JD Wetherspoon would ever switch to Coca-Cola, which provides some certainty to Carlsberg-Britvic’s soft drinks sales in the licensed channel.

The next territories?

Lastly, there is an open question around whether Carlsberg can both expand its strategic partnership with PepsiCo (alongside taking over UK Pepsi bottling, it will also take on Pepsi bottling in Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan) while continuing to bottle Coca-Cola in Denmark and Finland. Our understanding is that the Coca-Cola bottling licenses in Denmark and Finland are up for renewal in 2026.

As discussed in ‘[Super System](https://research.redburnatlantic.com/pdf/45236?title=Super%2520System)’ (8 September 2023), in the modern era there are few examples of TCCC not renewing a bottler’s contract for reasons other than broader System refranchising initiatives. The key exceptions were when bottlers competed against TCCC with their own products, such as Castel with World Cola in its African markets, which was significantly cheaper than the Coca-Cola Classic that it was also bottling. Castel’s Coca-Cola bottling agreement ended in 2022.

The problem for TCCC is that Carlsberg is a good partner in Denmark: it has the best distribution network, enormous local influence and longstanding relationships with the key customers.

Carlsberg took on Coca-Cola bottling in Denmark in 2002 after a deal to restructure the former 51:49 joint venture that it had with TCCC in the Nordics. From 2002-19 we estimate the Carlsberg’s Danish soft drinks volumes (which are predominantly comprised of TCCC’s brands) rose at a 4-5% CAGR (Fig 45). However, it is difficult to know whether this is a ‘clean’ growth rate as there was a thriving cross-border business between Denmark and northern Germany that encroached on CCEP’s sales growth. In 2020, TCCC stepped in and asked Carlsberg to cease this cross-border trade – a sign of TCCC’s strong alignment with CCEP, but for Carlsberg it meant that its Danish soft drinks volumes are still c15-20% lower than in 2019.

Sugar taxes, Extended Producer Responsibility and Deposit Return Schemes

The final topic to discuss in Great Britain is the increasingly burdensome regulatory environment for soft drinks companies, which could constrict the long-term margin expansion story for CCGB. Positively, however, it could also cement the market positions of the incumbents.

The three areas we follow closely are changes to the UK soft drinks industry levy (SDIL), the proposed Deposit Return Scheme due to start on 1 October 2027, and the changes being made to Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR).

Not so sweet

On 30 October 2024, the UK government announced that the SDIL rates will be increased annually (from 1 April 2025) across five years (from 2025 to 2030) to spread the burden of a 27% cumulative increase. This is to catchup on years of inflation since the levy was introduced in April 2018 – the levy was previously a fixed rate depending on the sugar content of the beverage and as a result, when soft drinks prices rose from 2022 as manufacturers passed on severe cost inflation, the levy fell as a proportion of the retail price of the soft drink. Beyond 2030, the SDIL rates will increase every year in line with the Consumer Price Index (CPI).

We assume that CCGB will fully pass on the levy increase each year, meaning that price/mix will receive a boost in 2025-30 (and beyond, albeit at a lower amount). There will also be gradual ongoing margin pressure as the levy raises CCGB’s revenue, but it does not impact the gross profit (as it is passed on to the UK government).

As it stands the levy would not affect the majority of CCGB’s portfolio – in 2024, 68% of volumes were from low- or no-calorie products (Fig 46). But the UK government is proposing to lower the threshold at which the levy applies from 5g of sugar per 100ml to 4g of sugar per 100ml.

Clearly this would be more beneficial to government coffers than it would be to public health. This proposed SDIL change could lead to more reformulations for CCGB, for example, Fanta in the UK has 4.5g of sugar per 100ml and Oasis has 4.0g of sugar per 100ml, below the current threshold but above the proposed future threshold. However, it is not clear that further reformulations would be a permanent solution as the government could decide to lower the threshold again in future. It is worth noting that regular Pepsi has 4.5g of sugar per 100ml in the UK and it could require a further reformulation, although as shown earlier in Fig 38 Carlsberg-Britvic is focused on sugar-free Pepsi Max.

The main issue that we see for CCGB is that if the UK government is looking for additional tax revenue then soft drinks (an sin taxes in general) is an easy target and we could see further changes, such as a tax on non-nutritive sweeteners, or energy drinks. As discussed in our recent report ‘[Resilience in Taxing Times](https://redburn.rothschildandco.com/pdf/75310?title=Resilience+in+Taxing+Times&pages=27)’ (28 October 2025) this is a theme across the global soft drinks landscape and, positively, CCEP already has plenty of experience successfully navigating sugar taxes and it operates in territories with higher-income consumers that are better able to weather the tax-induced price rise.

Disaster Rocks Soft drinks

To put it nicely, the UK’s Deposit Return Scheme (DRS) has so far been chaotic. The UK government initially proposed a DRS back in 2018. But there was not a joined-up approach between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, and each had their own timelines, policy design and legal frameworks. This would have created a nightmare for the soft drinks manufacturers.

For example, the Scotland DRS was initially due to launch in September 2022; nearly a year before the England DRS was supposed to begin in August 2023. The large manufacturers like CCGB, Britvic and AG Barr all operate supply chains that span across England and Scotland and would have had to design Scotland-specific packaging for a lone year. Meanwhile, Scottish consumers could travel across the border to England, buy cheaper bottles of their favourite soft drink, and then return them to reverse vending machines in Scotland to receive a deposit rebate.

There was also uncertainty around the mechanics of the DRS scheme, with the UK government initially proposing to charge VAT on unredeemed deposits. In the grand scheme this would have raised an immaterial amount of tax revenue, but it would have imposed a higher fee for consumers and created an accounting and packaging-tracking nightmare for the soft drinks manufacturers.

As it stands, a UK-wide DRS is due to be implemented on 1 October 2027. The deposit amount has not been confirmed yet by the scheme administrator – the Deposit Management Organisation (DMO) – but we expect it will be around 20p per single-use container, in line with other European countries that operate DRSs. Glass bottles are currently excluded from the UK DRS, but PET, aluminium and steel containers are all covered. The UK DRS covers any single-use vessel between 150ml to 3-litres, regardless of whether they are sold individually or as part of a multipack.

The soft drinks manufacturers will also be required to pay an ongoing producer fee, which once again has yet to be confirmed by the DMO. This fee will help to fund the rollout of reverse-vending machine infrastructure, as well as the ongoing costs to run the DRS. Analysis from DEFRA suggests the producer fee could amount to 4p per container in year one, falling to 3p per unit thereafter once the initial investment for the required infrastructure is completed[[5]](#footnote-6). Note, this fee does not raise the price of the beverage (unless the soft drinks manufacturers decide to pass it on to consumers) and instead it increases the manufacturer’s COGS.

What does the DRS mean for CCGB?

Using the company’s disclosure, our interpretation is that the DRS covers c85% of CCGB’s volumes (Fig 47). It is worth noting that Great Britain is an outlier in CCEP’s markets in terms of the proportion of volumes sold in aluminium cans.

The majority of these cans are in multipacks and the UK DRS looks as if it will cover each individual can within a multipack: the draft wording states it will cover “single-use drinks containers”. The issue is that an 8x 330ml can format effectively contains eight single-use containers, even though it is a multi-serve format.

More broadly, there is a risk that volumes across the soft drinks industry could fall following the retail price increases caused by the DRS. However, given the DRS will result in an industry-wide, one-off price increase, which also impacts other beverage categories like beer and spirits, we would expect the volume hit to be modest.

Indeed, a review performed by Reloop and the Container Recycling Institute[[6]](#footnote-7), which examined the impact of DRS implementation in numerous regions (Scandinavia, the Baltics, Germany, Australia etc), concluded that *“None of the case studies provide definitive evidence suggesting that the introduction or expansion of a DRS caused a decline in beverage sales”*.

For now, we await more information from the DMO regarding the specifics of the deposit amount and producer fee before updating of CCGB forecasts.

Neverending regulation

Finally, there is Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR). This has increased the reporting burden (and costs) for soft drinks manufacturers as they are required to register and report packaging tonnage data twice a year to the government’s online Report Packaging Data (RPD) portal. From January 2025, large manufacturers like CCGB were then charged a base fee for each tonne of packaging placed into the market, based on the material used, with the first invoice in October 2025 (Fig 49).

Note that the EPR fees for glass and aluminium have decreased from the draft rates that we discussed for Fever-Tree in ‘[Post FY24 results: RTDs and EPR](https://redburn.rothschildandco.com/pdf/66525?title=Post+FY24+results:+RTDs+and+EPR&pages=13)’ (1 April 2025), which were far more punitive (the draft rates from UK Department for Environment were originally £240 per tonne for glass and £435 per tonne for aluminium). The initial design was a bit odd given the purpose of EPR is to promote a circular economy, but as EPR is based on weight and glass is naturally heavy it was punished, even though glass can theoretically be recycled an infinite number of times and has a high collection rate, whereas PET bottles cannot and have a lower collection rate.

CCEP does not provide enough data to calculate the impact of EPR, but we expect that it will have a modest impact in Great Britain as glass is only c2% of volumes.

For reference, Fever-Tree estimates that EPR will add c£1.5m of costs (worth c1.4% of total UK sales) to its UK off-trade business and if EPR extends to the on-trade (which is an open debate with the UK government) this would add a further c£3m of costs (c2.7% of UK sales, bringing the total potential EPR costs to c4.1% of Fever-Tree’s UK sales). Fever-Tree is much more skewed towards glass than CCGB is, so we would expect that the EPR cost for CCGB will be lower as a percentage of sales.

From October 2026, EPR could become even more complicated with fee modulation under a new traffic-light grading system called Recyclability Assessment Methodology (RAM). Soft drinks manufacturers must then not only report the weight and material, but also the recyclability class (via RAM) and “nation data” i.e. in which UK nation was the packaging supplied in, and in which nation was the packaging discarded in.

We have no idea how this disposal data will be accurately collected in practice, but regardless, this will further increase the reporting burden for CCGB. It may also lead to packaging alterations to, for example, shift a pack from “amber” to “green” under the RAM system.

In conclusion, the regulatory burden of operating in the UK soft drinks market is set to increase dramatically. We expect the large incumbents like CCGB and Carlsberg-Britvic will be able to navigate these challenges and, perhaps positively, the rising regulatory burden could cement the position of the incumbents.

It will also be interesting to see how the grocers navigate these challenges with their private label offerings – the per unit ‘regulation cost’ will rise, knocking the already low profitability of their private label soft drinks. This means that the grocers could prune their low velocity private label SKUS, which could benefit the branded soft drinks manufacturers by freeing up shelf space.

Valuation

On FactSet consensus estimates CCEP trades on 17.6x one-year forward P/E, an 8% premium to the historical average of 16.3x, but a 5% discount to the company’s pre-pandemic rating of 18.5x (Fig 58). This is an attractive entry point given CCEP offers faster top- and bottom-line growth today compared to pre-pandemic (c4% “and more” organic sales growth pa today versus c3% pa pre-pandemic, and c7% organic profit growth pa versus c5% pa pre-pandemic) along with high free cash flows (at least €1.7bn pa today versus at least €1.25bn pa pre-pandemic).

On our above-consensus estimates, CCEP trades on 17.3x one-year forward P/E and offers a 10% EPS CAGR over the next three years, with a c3% dividend yield.

Historically, the Coca-Cola bottlers traded at a 10-20% discount to the consumer staples sector on one-year forward P/E, largely due to the perceived risk that TCCC could one day decide not to renew their bottling agreements, and due to the bottlers’ lower return on invested capital (ROIC) versus peers. As discussed in ‘[Super System](https://research.redburnatlantic.com/pdf/45236?title=Super%2520System)’ (8 September 2023), in reality the risk that TCCC would not renew the bottling agreement for CCEP – its largest bottler globally by sales and the largest contributor to TCCC’s operating income – is in our view remote. Likewise, CCEP’s 10.8% ROIC in 2024 is hampered relative to consumer staples peers by the accounting treatment of its franchise intangible assets.

Today, CCEP trades at a 9% premium to the sector on one-year forward P/E (Fig 59). We argue this premium is justified by CCEP’s faster EPS growth and relatively more favourable risk/reward profile versus the broader sector: it is not exposed to the potential headwinds facing the US consumer staples stocks, such as the near-term operational risks from tariffs and the longer-term threat of GLP-1 agonists on terminal growth rates. Moreover, in the near term CCEP has gross margin support in 2026 from easing European sugar and PET costs that we argue is not adequately reflected in consensus estimates, as well as longer-term margin upside if the AFH channel recovers to pre-pandemic levels.
